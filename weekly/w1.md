# Week 1 — User Research & Competitive Landscape

> **Status:** Draft for sign-off · Contributors: @sho6000, @YojitShinde
> **Source:** 10-question developer survey + 10-platform competitive scan

## Part 1 — Developer Survey

**Q1. Where do developers currently go to find answers when they're stuck?**
Most developers use AI tools first, followed by Stack Overflow, GitHub Issues, official documentation, Reddit, Discord, and colleagues.

**Q2. How important is it to know the experience or credibility of the person answering a question?**
Not much, developers simply want a solution that works for them.

**Q3. What makes a developer decide to ask another person instead of continuing to search?**
They seek human help mainly when AI-tools respond poorly and there is absence of existing answers.

**Q4. How long is a developer willing to wait for a response to his/her question?**
Most expect responses within minutes to a few hours, especially for blocking issues. (Maybe less)

**Q5. What would discourage a developer from responding to questions?**
Poor-quality questions, repetitive posts, notification overload, and lack of recognition discourage participation.

**Q6. What features would make developers trust a new technical Q&A platform?**
Verified experts, reputation scores, anti ai-slop and accurate answers are the most desired features.

**Q7. How would developers prefer to receive notifications for questions related to their expertise?**
Email notifications.

**Q8. What frustrates developers most about existing platforms like Stack Overflow, Reddit, Discord, or GitHub Discussions?**
Outdated answers, duplicate closures, and unreliable AI-generated responses.

**Q9. What features or improvements do developers wish existing Q&A platforms offered?**
Faster expert responses, Verified responders.

**Q10. What would convince a developer to regularly use a new platform for asking and answering technical questions?**
Reliable answers from verified experts — encourages asking.
Opportunity to increase your credibility — encourages answering.

## Part 2 — Competitive Landscape

| # | Platform | What it does | What it lacks |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Stack Overflow for Teams (SME) | Assigns subject matter experts to tags and notifies them of relevant questions. | Limited to private paid workspaces with no public expert recognition. |
| 2 | Stack Overflow / Stack Exchange | Lets users follow tags and earn reputation through answering questions. | No formal expert roles or automatic expert routing for tagged questions. |
| 3 | Experts Exchange | Connects users with experienced contributors for technical Q&A. | Uses a paywalled, outdated model without topic-based expert notifications. |
| 4 | GitHub CODEOWNERS | Automatically requests reviews from designated code owners for matching files. | Designed for code reviews rather than public question-and-answer discussions. |
| 5 | Qiskit Bot | Notifies predefined experts when documentation related to their expertise changes. | Works only within a single project and requires manual expert assignment. |
| 6 | Codenotify (Sourcegraph) | Allows developers to subscribe to file changes without requiring reviews. | Focuses only on code notifications and lacks public expert discovery. |
| 7 | Discord Reaction Roles | Lets users self-assign topic roles that others can mention for help. | Provides no reputation system or intelligent expert matching. |
| 8 | HARO / Qwoted | Matches topic experts with journalists seeking expert opinions. | Does not build a public knowledge base or reputation system. |
| 9 | Quora | Allows users to follow topics and showcase self-declared expertise. | Lacks verified expertise and real-time expert notifications. |
| 10 | Reddit | Uses flairs and karma to recognize contributors within communities. | Does not notify experts when relevant questions are posted. |

**The gap common to all ten:** nobody routes a question to a verified topic expert and gives them recognition they can carry outside the platform.

## Part 3 — Contradictions 

### C1. Q2 against Q6, Q9 and Q10

Q2 says credibility barely matters. Q6, Q9 and Q10 all name verified experts as the most wanted feature. These cannot both be taken at face value.

Likely reconciliation: credibility doesn't matter *once you can see the answer works*. It's a prediction device people use before they can evaluate — deciding whether an answer is worth trusting, or whether waiting is worth it. It is not something they want displayed.

**Why it matters:** if that reading holds, verification is routing metadata, and badge design is a much smaller job than it looks. If it doesn't hold, badges are user-facing chrome and need real design effort. Different products.

**To settle it:** ask respondents whether they would wait longer for an answer if they knew a verified expert was working on it. If yes, verification is about the wait, not the badge.

- the credibility part is for recognition that can be shared in other platforms, starting the platfrom with few will be an issue for sometime, but later on people can validate themselves, (when someone asks questions, anyone can respond to that, person who asked a question can rate each and everyones responce, based on that they can level up.) there is no waiting, when someone answers/helps a question, they question giver can rate or whichever responce seems helpful, they can rate it and that rating eventually turns into points for that responder and eventually accumulate to become levels.

### C2. Q4 against Q7

Q4 says minutes to hours, especially when blocked. Q7 says email. Email is an hours-latency channel — it cannot deliver minutes.

Likely reconciliation: people chose email because they are tired of chat noise, which Q5 confirms. The real preference is *low-noise*, not *email specifically*.

**Why it matters:** this decides the notification architecture outright. Email-only means accepting hours and designing the asker's wait experience around it. A faster channel means solving noise control instead.

**To settle it:** ask whether they would accept a faster channel if it were strictly capped in volume — say, three notifications a day maximum.

### C3. Q6 against Q3 and Q8

Q6 wants anti-AI-slop as a core feature. But Q3 says people arrive here precisely because AI failed them, and Q8 names unreliable AI answers as a top frustration.

The tension is in the solution, not the desire. Automated AI-content detectors have high false-positive rates, and wrongly flagging a human answerer hits the recognition problem Q5 names as a churn driver. Accusing a real contributor of being a bot is a good way to lose them permanently.

**Why it matters:** it determines whether we build a detector at all. The alternative is structural — people came here *because* AI failed, so posting AI output is self-defeating, and voting plus reporting may be sufficient.

## Part 4 — What Needs Addressing

### G1. The survey is asker-weighted

Eight of ten questions are about asking. Only Q5 and Q10 touch answering.

The answerer is the side that decides whether this works. Marketplaces die on supply, not demand — askers arrive on their own once answers exist; answerers do not. We are designing for a person we have two data points about.

**Action:** ten short conversations with people who currently answer questions in public — in Discord, on GitHub, in Stack Overflow. One question each: *what would make you answer here instead of where you already do?*

### G2. Stated preference, not observed behaviour

A survey captures what people say when asked in the abstract. C2 is the clearest symptom — "email" is the tidy answer to a hypothetical question, and it contradicts the urgency in Q4.

**Action:** three workflow observations. Watch someone actually get unblocked. Where do they go first, second, third, and what makes them give up on searching?

### G3. "Verified" is undefined

Q6 and Q9 both want verified responders. The competitive scan says every incumbent gets this wrong. Neither tells us what right looks like.

Self-declared, peer-vouched, credential-linked, or earned in-platform are four different products.

**Action:** ask directly. *What would make you believe someone actually knows this topic?*

### G4. Nothing on willingness to lose a role

Q5 tells us what discourages answering. Nothing tells us how people would react to losing a topic role through inactivity or poor responses.

A role nobody can lose isn't a signal — but a role that can be lost may deter people from taking it at all. We need to know which risk is larger.

**Action:** include in the ten answerer conversations.

### G5. Sample size and composition unrecorded

We have not written down how many people responded, how they were recruited, or their experience distribution. Findings without a denominator are hard to weigh later, and impossible for a third contributor to assess.

**Action:** document it. If the sample is small or narrow, say so plainly rather than letting the numbers read as more solid than they are.

## Part 5 — Personas

Two, not one. The sides have opposing incentives, and collapsing them into a single persona hides the conflict the routing policy exists to resolve.

### Persona A — The Answerer (primary design target)

Senior engineer, several years deep in one or two domains. Employed full-time; this is not her job. Already answers informally in a private work channel nobody outside can see, which means her best work leaves no public trace.

She would answer here for recognition that means something outside the platform (Q10). Not money.

She abandons over notification volume (Q5) — quietly, by muting, not by complaining. That is how she left Discord and she will leave here the same way.

**What success looks like:** pinged a couple of times a week, both times about something in her actual specialism, answers in ten minutes, and six months later has something she can point to.

### Persona B — The Asker (constraint set, not design target)

Two to four years in, blocked, and already failed by an AI and by search (Q1, Q3). Coming to a human is his third move, not his first.

This means every question arriving has survived AI and search. **They are hard by selection** — which lowers the value of duplicate detection and raises the cost of every answer.

He needs a response inside hours because he is blocked (Q4). He abandons after asking twice and getting nothing twice.

### The conflict to design around

| The Answerer needs | The Asker needs | Where they collide |
|---|---|---|
| Few, high-quality pings | To be seen fast | Fewer pings means slower coverage |
| Questions worth her time | To ask when stuck, not when articulate | Quality gates add friction at the worst moment |
| Durable recognition | An answer, then to leave | The person building the reputation system rarely uses it |

The routing policy is where this conflict gets resolved. It is the first thing to design.
